I didn't set out to become a test engineer. I set out to understand **signals**: how information survives noise, distortion, and interference well enough to still mean something on the other end. That was the whole point of a BSc in Electronic Engineering in Thessaloniki, and later an MSc in Digital Signal Processing in Cardiff: find the signal, filter the noise, prove it holds up under conditions you don't control.

Then came a stretch that, on paper, looks like a detour: supervising a university coffee shop, then working as a photographer and founding the Cardiff Photography Club. It grew past 250 members in its first year, not because I had a five-year plan, but because I kept showing up, week after week, and paid attention to what people actually needed from it. No single event built that club. A **repeatable habit** did.

Looking back, that wasn't a break from the engineering mindset, it was the same one pointed somewhere else. Photography is signal processing with a camera: deciding what's noise and what's worth keeping, constantly, under conditions you don't fully control. Running a club is the same **discipline** applied to people instead of pixels, watching what breaks, what people actually use, what quietly stops working if nobody's paying attention.

## Back to something that looked like the start

In 2017 I became **ISTQB certified** and started as a QA Tester, then Test Analyst, at Computershare, writing BDD feature files, building regression suites, learning the discipline of quality from the ground up like it was a first job, not a ninth year of one.

It didn't feel like starting over. It felt like the same question I'd been asking since Thessaloniki, just with a more honest name: where exactly does this break, and how do I prove it before someone else finds out the hard way? A **regression suite**, in the end, is just a habit made executable: the same small check, run the same way, every single time, so nobody has to trust memory or good intentions.

From there: core banking systems and a first serious end-to-end automation project at Hodge Bank, then building performance and component test frameworks from scratch at Currencycloud (now Visa), **root-causing defects** across FX trading flows that mattered because real money moved on the other side of them.

## The unglamorous part is the whole point

I'm currently finishing a BSc in Computing & IT part-time, alongside full-time work, not in dramatic bursts, but in whatever hour is actually available that week. It's the same logic I keep coming back to in *Atomic Habits*, one of the few books that's stuck with me: **tiny, repeatable systems** beat big one-off efforts, and **identity-based change** ("I'm someone who tests assumptions, who keeps a habit going") holds up far better than ambitious, short-lived goals ever do. The **AI tooling** I use now fits the same pattern: not a shortcut around the discipline, just a newer tool for the same old habit of not trusting anything until you've tried to break it, one small check at a time.

If there's a thread running through electronics, photography, and testing, it's that one: **small, boring, repeatable** beats big and occasional, every time. Everything else, the job titles, the tools, the years in between, is just where that habit happened to land.
